\begin{abstract}
% ------------------------------------------------
Hindi encodes honorific level through pronouns (\aap{}/\tum{}/\tu{}) and verb
agreement. We ask whether Gemma-2-2B represents this level before any such
marker appears, and whether public Gemma Scope SAEs expose a feature that
captures it. We build a 600-pair contrastive Hindi set with a
\emph{context-only, pre-marker} condition: the input describes the social
setting but contains no second-person pronoun or agreement-bearing form. On this
condition, a linear probe on a middle layer reaches AUROC $0.722$, against
$0.610$ for word TF--IDF, $0.550$ for character $n$-grams, and $0.535$ at
layer~0. The signal holds with addressee roles held out ($0.692$) and on
naturalistic prefixes ($0.655$). Because the contexts state the
speaker--addressee relation, and the probe is binary (\aap{} versus
\tum{}/\tu{}), this shows that register-relevant social context is linearly
decodable, not that the model holds a dedicated honorific variable.

The SAE analysis is negative. At layer~2, none of 50 candidates ranked by raw
effect size passes our prespecified pronoun-specificity audit, and an
exploratory five-prompt intervention on the top candidate shows no dose response
or necessity. We therefore frame the work as a probing and negative-results
study: it does not localise honorific information in the SAE dictionary or
identify a causal handle. We release the dataset, rubric, and a single-GPU
pipeline.
\end{abstract}

% ------------------------------------------------
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ------------------------------------------------

Hindi honorific choice is both socially consequential and grammatically
constrained. Addressing an elder with \tu{} instead of \aap{}, or pairing \aap{}
with a singular verb (\textit{karta hai}) where plural-respectful agreement
(\textit{karte hain}) is required, produces a mismatch in register and
agreement \citep{kachru2006hindi}. Whether multilingual language models
represent honorific level internally, and where, is largely unknown.

\paragraph{Research questions.} We ask three questions.
\textbf{RQ1}: does a linear probe on Gemma-2 residual streams distinguish
Hindi honorific level \emph{when the pronoun and explicit agreement marker are
absent}? \textbf{RQ2}: which Gemma Scope SAE features keep honorific
specificity once both surface channels are audited? \textbf{RQ3}: does a
tested candidate feature satisfy the causal triangle of correlation, steering
sufficiency, and ablation necessity? A lighter, probe-only comparison with
Japanese keigo and a native-speaker evaluation are reported in
Section~\ref{sec:supplementary}.

\paragraph{What this study can say about ``where''.}
The title asks where politeness lives, and our evidence answers only part of
that. The layer-wise probe shows at which depth honorific-relevant context is
most linearly accessible. The SAE search, by contrast, covers a single early
layer, and the intervention covers a single feature. We therefore treat the
paper as a probing and negative-results study: the SAE and intervention
experiments show what a raw effect-size search does and does not recover, and
they do not locate an honorific representation.

\paragraph{Motivation.}
Gemma Scope \citep{lieberum2024gemmascope} provides pretrained SAEs at every
Gemma-2 layer, which makes a targeted study feasible on one GPU. Probing work
shows that multilingual models share cross-lingual structure
\citep{pires2019multilingual} and that grammatical structure can be recovered
from word representations \citep{hewitt2019structural}. Whether social register
is accessible in the same way has not been tested for Hindi.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep0pt
  \item A pre-marker probing result. Before any pronoun or explicit agreement
        marker is realised, a middle-layer probe reaches AUROC $0.722$,
        against $0.610$ for word TF--IDF, $0.550$ for character $n$-grams, and
        $0.535$ at layer~0. The result transfers to held-out addressee roles
        ($0.692$) and naturalistic pre-marker prefixes ($0.655$). We state
        explicitly what it does not show (Section~\ref{sec:context-results}).
  \item A prespecified SAE specificity audit with a negative outcome: none of 50
        raw-effect-size-ranked candidates satisfies
        $A_{\mathrm{hon}}\geq0.80$ and $A_{\mathrm{pron}}\leq0.55$ on
        pronoun-stripped pairs.
  \item An exploratory intervention on the top-ranked candidate, reported as a
        failure case rather than a causal test, since the audit shows that the
        candidate is a pronoun detector.
  \item A 600-pair contrastive Hindi honorific dataset (CC-BY-SA) with a
        context-only condition for every pair, a 200-pair Japanese keigo
        analogue, a six-axis rubric rated by three native Hindi speakers on 192
        generations, and a reproducible pipeline for a single 24\,GB GPU.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}
\label{sec:related}
% ------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Hindi honorifics.}
Hindi marks honorific register through two co-occurring grammatical mechanisms
\citep{kachru2006hindi}. The first is the \emph{second-person pronoun}: \aap{}
(formal and respectful, used with elders, strangers, and superiors), \tum{}
(familiar, used with peers), and \tu{} (intimate or dismissive). The second is
\emph{verb agreement}: plural forms (\textit{karte hain}, \textit{gaye the})
obligatorily co-occur with \aap{}, while singular forms (\textit{karta hai})
co-occur with \tu{} and typically with \tum. The honorific level of an
utterance is therefore encoded jointly by pronoun and verb agreement.
Japanese contrasts with this: polite and plain verbal morphology (e.g.,
\textit{-masu} versus the plain form) is a central realisation of politeness
\citep{shibatani1990languages}, and the keigo system also includes
\textit{sonkeigo} (referent-elevating) and \textit{kenj\=ogo} (self-lowering)
forms. Our Japanese comparison covers only the polite versus plain contrast.

\paragraph{Honorifics are not general formality.} We use \emph{honorific}
strictly for the morphosyntactically encoded speaker--addressee relation. A
formal news headline has no honorific level, and a casual request to a close
friend has a low one. The contrastive pairs, confound controls
(Section~\ref{sec:confounds}), and rubric (Section~\ref{sec:causal-method}) are
designed to isolate this distinction.

\paragraph{The pronoun-token confound.}
Because \aap{}, \tum{}, and \tu{} are highly informative single tokens, a
feature that fires on pronoun identity is not evidence of abstract honorific
encoding. A pronoun-stripped probe addresses this risk at the probe level.
Feature-level specificity needs separate, orientation-invariant honorific and
pronoun AUCs, so we never infer specificity from effect size alone.

\paragraph{Sparse autoencoders.}
An SAE trained on a transformer residual stream learns an over-complete
dictionary of feature directions and decomposes each activation as a sparse
positive combination \citep{templeton2024scaling}. Feature steering adds
$+\alpha\mathbf{v}_f$, for decoder direction $\mathbf{v}_f$, to the residual
stream at inference, and ablation subtracts it. Gemma
Scope \citep{lieberum2024gemmascope} releases pretrained JumpReLU SAEs at all
layers of Gemma-2-2B and Gemma-2-9B.

\paragraph{Interpretability of multilingual and grammatical features.}
Interpretability work studies knowledge through probing
\citep{alain2017understanding,hewitt2019structural}, causal analysis
\citep{geiger2022causalmedianalysis}, circuit discovery \citep{conmy2023automated},
and representation editing \citep{meng2022locating}. The closest multilingual
work identifies, ablates, and steers with SAE-derived language-specific
features \citep{deng2025languagespecific}; Zwirner et al.\ evaluate such
steering across tasks and models \citep{zwirner2026evaluating}. TenseLoC
combines probing, causal tracing, and SAE steering for grammatical tense in
eight languages, including Hindi \citep{tumurchuluun2025tenseloc}. Tense is a
single-slot morphological choice tied to an objective time reference. Hindi
honorific register instead requires coordinating a pronoun with matching verb
agreement across a generation, and it depends on a socially conditioned
judgment of addressee status. This is why it is unclear that a single decoded
or steered direction could control it.

\paragraph{Honorifics in NLP.}
Prior work tests Korean mismatch surprisal \citep{noh2024honorific} and
sociopragmatic constraints \citep{cho2026deference}, and machine translation
work controls politeness at the output level \citep{sennrich2016controlling}.
We are not aware of earlier work that applies an orientation-invariant
pronoun-confound audit to SAE features for an Indic honorific system. The
closed pronoun set makes pronoun-token detection a concrete, checkable
confound for raw effect-size ranking (Section~\ref{sec:feature-id}), and the
same audit could be reused for other closed-class address-term systems such as
Japanese, Korean, or Javanese.

\subsection{Data and Models}
\label{sec:dataset}

\paragraph{Models.}
We use Gemma-2-2B (${\approx}5$\,GB in bf16; Gemma Scope JumpReLU SAEs at all 26
layers). The model plus one layer SAE peaks at $14.4$\,GB of VRAM in our runs, which
fits on a single 24\,GB RTX~4500~Ada. We use \texttt{transformer-lens} for
residual-stream extraction \citep{nanda2022transformerlens} and
\texttt{sae-lens} for SAE loading \citep{bloom2024saelens}.

\paragraph{Hindi contrastive pairs.}
We construct 600 minimally contrastive Hindi sentence pairs. The two members
of a pair share propositional content and differ in honorific level (\aap{} vs.\
\tum, \aap{} vs.\ \tu, or \tum{} vs.\ \tu). Items span six domains: social,
professional, family, public service, medical, and customer service. Each item
comes with the following conditions (Table~\ref{tab:example} illustrates the
format):

\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
  \item \textbf{Raw condition}: the completed sentence, including pronoun and
        agreeing verb.
  \item \textbf{Pronoun-stripped condition}: a variant with all second-person
        pronoun tokens removed, leaving verb agreement and context intact. This
        isolates honorific signal from pronoun-token identity.
  \item \textbf{Context-only, pre-marker condition}: a paired social scenario
        that specifies the speaker--addressee relation and the intended content,
        and ends before any realised target pronoun or agreement-bearing form. We
        extract the residual state at the final context token and ask whether it
        predicts the register of the next utterance. This is the primary
        condition for RQ1.
\end{itemize}

Members of a pair differ only in the honorific marker, so length and content
are matched within a pair by construction. The first author, a native Hindi
speaker, wrote the pairs from minimal-pair templates that vary only the
honorific marker (the pronoun and the agreeing verb form). Honorific labels
follow deterministically from the template, so no separate annotation pass was
needed. The author checked naturalness on a held-out sample.

\paragraph{Japanese comparison set.}
The 200 pairs contrast \textit{desu/masu} with plain forms and mirror the
Hindi minimal-pair design: each pair differs only in polite versus plain verbal
morphology.

\subsection{Probing}
\label{sec:probing-method}

For completed-sentence diagnostics we extract residual-stream activations at all
26 layers and mean-pool over token positions. For the context-only evaluation
we instead take the residual state at the final context token, before generation
begins. We train L2-regularised logistic regression probes ($C=1.0$;
\citealt{scikit-learn}) and report 5-fold cross-validated AUROC, grouped by pair
identifier, on the raw, pronoun-stripped, and context-only sets. The primary
claim rests on the context-only set, because its input ends before both direct
morphological cues. We also evaluate a matched character $n$-gram baseline, a
word TF--IDF baseline, a split that holds out addressee roles, and naturalistic
prefixes that end before an overt marker.

\paragraph{Binary label.}
The probe is binary: \aap{}-class versus \tum{}/\tu{}-class. This means that in a
\tum{} versus \tu{} pair both members receive the same label, so such pairs
carry no binary contrast. Our results therefore support claims about \aap{}
versus non-\aap{} only. They say nothing about whether the model separates
\tum{} from \tu{}, and a three-class or ordinal probe is left for future work.

\subsection{SAE Candidate Search and Audit}
\label{sec:feature-id}

We use the Gemma Scope residual-stream SAE (16k features, JumpReLU,
$\ell_0 = 53$) at layer~2, the SAE layer available for our feature-search
pilot. This is an operational choice: layer~2 maximised the pronoun-stripped
completed-sentence probe, whose layer profile is nearly flat. It is not evidence
that layer~2 is a privileged or representative mechanistic locus, and it is not
the layer at which the context-only probe performs best. The feature-level analysis is restricted
to this SAE and layer. For each feature $f$ we compute Cohen's~$d$ on the raw
condition:

\begin{equation}
  d_f = \frac{\mu_{\aap}(f) - \mu_{\tum/\tu}(f)}{\sigma_\text{pooled}(f)}
  \label{eq:cohend}
\end{equation}

Candidates are ranked by $d_f$. To test pronoun-independent specificity without
directional ambiguity, we then compute two orientation-invariant quantities:
\begin{equation}
 A_c(f)=\tfrac{1}{2}+\left|\mathrm{AUC}_c(f)-\tfrac{1}{2}\right|,
\end{equation}
with $c\in\{\mathrm{hon},\mathrm{pron}\}$. Here $A_{\mathrm{hon}}$ is honorific
discrimination on pronoun-stripped pairs and $A_{\mathrm{pron}}$ is
raw-versus-stripped sentence discrimination. The prespecified passing rule is
$A_{\mathrm{hon}}\geq0.80$ and $A_{\mathrm{pron}}\leq0.55$. Because ranking uses
completed (raw) sentences, the audit tests whether this particular ranking
recovers pronoun-independent candidates. It is not an exhaustive search of the
SAE dictionary.

\subsection{Intervention and Evaluation}
\label{sec:causal-method}

We evaluate the three legs of the causal triangle
\citep{geiger2022causalmedianalysis} for one candidate, the top-ranked feature
$f_{646}$. The audit (Section~\ref{sec:rq2-results}) shows that this feature
fails the specificity criterion, so the intervention should be read as a
failure-case check on a pronoun-related feature. It is not a test of a validated
honorific feature.

\noindent\textbf{Correlation}: probe AUROC and per-feature effect size above.\\
\textbf{Sufficiency}: inject $+\alpha\mathbf{v}_f$ at layer~2 on naturally
\tum-form prompts and measure the shift in addressee honorificity.\\
\textbf{Necessity}: inject $-\alpha\mathbf{v}_f$ at layer~2 on naturally \aap-form
prompts and measure the drop in honorificity. We also run encode-side
zero-ablation, which zeroes the SAE encoding of feature $f$.

The decoder direction is unit-normalised before injection, so $\alpha$ is the
magnitude added in residual-norm units, comparable across features and layers.
Injection applies to generated positions only, which leaves the prompt context
intact. We sweep $\alpha \in \{2, 4, 8, 16\}$.

\paragraph{Scope of the pilot.} The sweep covers feature $f_{646}$ at layer~2
on five informal prompts, with one generation per prompt at each $\alpha$;
ablations use five naturally formal prompts. This is an exploratory diagnostic.
The sample is too small for stable confidence intervals or broad claims about
generation.

\paragraph{Honorific proxy.}
Each generation is scored by a deterministic morphological proxy,
$\mathrm{hon}(x) = (n_{\aap} - n_{\tu}) / n_{\text{tok}}$. Here $n_{\aap}$
counts \aap-paradigm pronouns and their agreeing plural-respectful verb forms
(\textit{hain}, \textit{kijiye}, \textit{karte}, \ldots), $n_{\tu}$ counts
\tu-paradigm pronouns and singular agreement forms, and $n_{\text{tok}}$ is the
token count. The proxy ranges over ${\sim}[-1,1]$, is easy to reproduce, and
operationalises the addressee-honorificity axis of the rubric.

\paragraph{Human rubric.}
Table~\ref{tab:rubric} shows the six-axis rubric used by the native-speaker
raters. Separating addressee honorificity from verb-agreement consistency
exposes a common failure mode in which the pronoun is correct but the agreement
is not.

\subsection{RQ1: Contextual Honorific Signal Before Overt Marking}
\label{sec:context-results}

A high probe AUROC on completed sentences could simply reflect Hindi's overt
plural-respectful agreement. We therefore treat the context-only, pre-marker
condition as the primary test: its input ends before a second-person pronoun or
explicit agreement marker is realised. Table~\ref{tab:context-probe} shows that
a character $n$-gram classifier, a strong surface-form baseline, is close to
chance (AUROC $0.550$), as is the layer-0 probe ($0.535$). Word TF--IDF reaches
$0.610$. The best middle-layer L2 probe reaches $0.722$, which is $0.187$ AUROC
above layer~0, $0.172$ above character $n$-grams, and $0.112$ above word
TF--IDF.

The signal is not confined to one split. It reaches $0.692$ when addressee roles
are held out and $0.655$ on naturalistic prefixes that end before an overt
marker. These evaluations narrow the morphology-only account, but they do not
isolate an honorific variable. The contexts state the speaker--addressee
relation, so a model that merely represents social roles such as elder, friend,
or supervisor would be expected to support a probe of this kind without any
separate honorific representation. We did not measure how many items can be
solved from role or scenario identity alone, and character $n$-gram and TF--IDF
baselines are weak stand-ins for semantic role information. We therefore read
the result as linear decodability of register-relevant social context.
Section~\ref{sec:failure} lists the controls that would sharpen it.

\subsection{RQ2: Raw-Ranked SAE Features Fail the Specificity Audit}
\label{sec:rq2-results}

We rank candidates by raw-condition Cohen's $d$ and audit all 50 with the
prespecified criterion: $A_{\mathrm{hon}}\geq0.80$ on pronoun-stripped pairs and
$A_{\mathrm{pron}}\leq0.55$ for raw-versus-stripped sentence discrimination.
No candidate passes. The top candidate, $f_{646}$, has a large raw effect size
($d=2.209$) but $A_{\mathrm{hon}}=0.514$ and $A_{\mathrm{pron}}=0.808$.
This is the failure mode one expects when pronoun identity is highly
predictive: the ranking surfaces pronoun-related features, not a validated
honorific feature. The result does not show that no such feature exists
elsewhere in the dictionary, at another layer, or under a search conducted on
pre-marker representations.

\subsection{RQ3: An Exploratory Intervention on a Failed Candidate}
\label{sec:rq3-results}

Because $f_{646}$ already fails the audit, this pilot is not a test of a
validated honorific feature. It asks only whether the highest-effect-size
candidate moves honorific marking. We inject the unit-normalised decoder
direction at layer~2, at generated positions only, on five informal prompts with
one generation per prompt and dose. The morphology proxy is bounded
approximately in $[-1,1]$. Table~\ref{tab:causal} shows a non-monotonic dose
response: the largest pre-collapse shift is only $+0.028$ at $\alpha=8$, and
$\alpha=16$ reverses the direction. On five naturally formal prompts, both
negative-direction and encode-side zero ablation preserve positive marking
instead of reducing it. These results support neither sufficiency nor
necessity, and the sample is too small for inferential statistics.

Figure~\ref{fig:probe} shows the layer-wise curves (5-fold grouped CV, with
both members of each contrastive pair held on the same side of every split to
prevent direct pair-member leakage; related template families may cross folds).
The raw condition reaches AUROC $= 0.894$ and the

dip around layer~12 before recovering. We therefore find no evidence that
\emph{linear separability} increases or becomes localized with depth, and we do
not interpret the argmax. Residual connections can preserve early surface
information while later layers compute additional abstractions, and AUROC may
also be near saturation. Flat probing therefore does not show that an abstract,
nonlinear, or circuit-level honorific representation is absent.

alone neither identifies that direction nor rules out other abstract features.

\paragraph{The evaluated candidate lacks a calibrated dose response.}
With the corrected steering (unit-normalised direction, residual-norm-scaled
$\alpha$, injection on generated positions only), generation stays coherent
Hindi--ruling out the off-manifold collapse seen with naive large-$\alpha$
injection--but the top feature does not controllably move honorific marking.
On \tum/\tu-form prompts the honorific proxy rises by at most $0.028$ (at
$\alpha{=}8$) and then \emph{reverses} at $\alpha{=}16$ as fluency degrades
(Table~\ref{tab:causal}); on \aap-form prompts, suppressing the feature does not
reduce honorific marking. Sufficiency is therefore negligible and necessity is
not observed under these ablations. This is consistent with the corrected audit
(Section~\ref{sec:rq2-results}): $f_{646}$ correlates with the honorific label
because it detects pronoun presence, not because it is an abstracted honorific
feature, so a coordinated pronoun${+}$agreement program is not something it
encodes to begin with. Since none of the other 49 ranked candidates passes the
specificity criterion either, this bounded negative result is not an artifact
of selecting the wrong feature among otherwise-valid honorific candidates.


Across prompts, below the collapse threshold steered generations are unchanged;
above it they degenerate. The feature behaves as a \emph{descriptive label
correlate}, not a reliable \emph{control handle} for register under this protocol.

\paragraph{No tested additive direction controls register--but prompting does.}
We test whether the failure is specific to a single feature or general to
additive steering, and whether the task is achievable at all
(Table~\ref{tab:baselines}). Combining features helps only marginally: the
top-20 combined direction reaches $\Delta = +0.093$, over four times the single
feature yet still inside the \tu-region (proxy $-0.006$, never flipping to
\aap). The \textbf{supervised difference-in-means direction}--the standard
activation-addition handle, computed directly from \aap{} vs.\ \tum/\tu{}
residuals--has a near-zero shift of $-0.003$ whose interval includes zero. By
contrast, 4-shot prompting flips the proxy to $+0.068$ ($\Delta = +0.166$,
producing genuine
\aap-form output such as \textit{``aap kahan se aaye hain?''}), and steering a
\emph{random} feature does nothing ($-0.031$, like the candidate). Two
conclusions follow. First, the negative result is \emph{not} a weak-model or
broken-proxy artefact: Gemma-2-2B can be driven to honorific Hindi and our
metric registers it. Second, the failure holds across the \emph{tested additive
residual-stream directions at this layer}--supervised and unsupervised, single
and multi-feature. Control
that works (prompting) operates through the model's computation, not an injected
vector.

Across 10,000 prompt-paired bootstrap resamples, top-20 steering remains below
prompting: their 95\% intervals are $[+0.084,+0.102]$ and
$[+0.157,+0.176]$, respectively. The supervised interval
$[-0.008,+0.001]$ contains zero.

\paragraph{Human ratings.}
Three native Hindi speakers, blinded to condition, rated 192 matched
generations across eight conditions (24 prompts each) on the six 0--3 rubric

establish population-scale pragmatic validity.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:failure}
% ------------------------------------------------

The probe, SAE, and intervention results constrain the possible accounts, but
they do not identify an internal mechanism.

\paragraph{What the probing result supports.}
Honorific-relevant information is linearly decodable from the residual stream
before any pronoun or agreement marker appears, and it beats surface baselines
and the layer-0 representation. This goes beyond simple marker recognition. The
remaining alternative is contextual correlation: topic, lexical choice, syntax,
and especially the explicitly stated social roles may predict honorific level
without any dedicated honorific variable. The held-out-role and
naturalistic-prefix results reduce this concern but do not remove it. Linear
decodability also does not show that the model uses the decoded direction when
it generates. The mechanistic conclusion we think the evidence supports is
therefore modest: register-relevant social context is accessible to a linear
readout in the middle of the network.

\paragraph{What we can and cannot say about ``where''.}
The probe indicates that context-only decodability is strongest at a middle
layer. Our SAE search ran at layer~2, which was an operational choice, so the
probing and SAE analyses are not aligned, and we cannot say which SAE features,
if any, correspond to the probed signal. Moreover, candidates were ranked on
completed sentences, although the main result concerns the pre-marker condition.
This ranking favours features that respond to overt pronouns, and all 50
candidates failed the audit, with the top feature at $A_{\mathrm{hon}}=0.514$
versus $A_{\mathrm{pron}}=0.808$. The experiment therefore shows that raw
effect-size ranking is confounded by pronoun identity. It does not test whether
an honorific feature exists at the probe-maximising layers or under a search run
directly on pre-marker representations.

\paragraph{Reading the intervention.}
The steering sweep is non-monotonic, and both ablations preserve formal marking.
It uses one feature, one layer, five prompts, and one generation per prompt and
dose, and the feature had already failed the specificity criterion. We treat it
as a failure case that illustrates why a feature chosen by effect size should
not be steered without an audit. It is not evidence for or against a causal
honorific representation. Producing honorific Hindi also requires coordinated
pronoun choice, verb agreement, and cross-clausal consistency, so a convincing
test needs a larger design than ours.

\paragraph{What would settle the question.}
Four extensions would turn this study into a mechanistic one.
(i)~Three-class or ordinal probing, to cover \tum{} versus \tu{}.
(ii)~Counterfactual role reversals in which the content is fixed and only the
social relation changes, with independently written contexts and a semantic
role baseline, to separate honorific information from role semantics.
(iii)~An SAE search that ranks candidates on pre-marker representations, applies
the same audit, and sweeps layers including the probe-maximising ones.
(iv)~Intervention on features that pass the audit, with more prompts, multiple
decoding seeds, and confidence intervals. A pre-marker Japanese evaluation would
also show whether the grammatical locus of marking changes what is decodable.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ------------------------------------------------

We studied how Gemma-2-2B and Gemma Scope SAEs represent Hindi honorific level.
After a strict pre-marker control, a middle-layer probe reaches AUROC $0.722$,
above word TF--IDF ($0.610$), character $n$-grams ($0.550$), and layer~0
($0.535$), with supporting held-out-role ($0.692$) and naturalistic-prefix
($0.655$) results. This shows that register-relevant social context is linearly
decodable, but not that the model holds a dedicated honorific representation:
the contexts state the social roles, and the probe is binary. None of 50
raw-ranked SAE candidates passes the pronoun-specificity audit, and a
five-prompt pilot on the top candidate shows neither a monotonic steering
response nor ablation necessity. We thus separate contextual decodability from
SAE-feature validation and causal control, and we leave localisation open. We
release the dataset, pre-marker controls, rubric, and analysis pipeline, which
runs on a single 24\,GB GPU.

\paragraph{Future work.}
Beyond the extensions in Section~\ref{sec:failure}, natural next steps include
Bengali, Tamil, and Marathi, graded register axes, instruction-tuned Gemma-2
variants, and a test of whether Japanese keigo's more local marking is easier to
steer than Hindi's coordinated system.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.}
Code, the 600-pair dataset (CC-BY-SA), the rubric, per-layer probe results, and
candidate feature indices accompany this paper as supplementary material and
are available at \url{https://github.com/amethystani/lihaaz}.


\section*{Limitations}
\textbf{Task definition.} The probe is binary (\aap{} versus \tum{}/\tu{}), so
\tum{}-versus-\tu{} pairs receive identical labels and carry no contrast. We
make no claim about three-level or ordinal honorific structure.

\textbf{Social-role confound.} The context-only inputs state the
speaker--addressee relation. A model that represents roles such as elder,
friend, or supervisor, without a separate honorific variable, could produce the
same result. We did not measure how many items are solvable from role or
scenario identity alone, and the dataset is structured and template-based. The
held-out-role and naturalistic-prefix evaluations are meaningful controls, not
substitutes for counterfactual role reversals, independently written contexts,
or open-domain validation.

\textbf{SAE and layer alignment.} The SAE analysis uses a single early layer
(layer~2) that does not coincide with the best probe layer. The two-way audit
covers 50 candidates ranked on raw sentences, and it cannot rule out other
features, feature combinations, layers, or nonlinear mechanisms.

\textbf{Intervention.} The steering and ablation pilot covers one feature that
had already failed the audit, one layer, five prompts per condition, and one
generation per prompt. It does not support confidence intervals, multi-seed
robustness, or claims about fluency and content preservation.

\textbf{Scope.} The primary probe, SAE, and intervention work uses Gemma-2-2B.
A probe-only replication covers nine models (supplementary), but SAE and causal
conclusions remain restricted to Gemma Scope. The Japanese comparison is
probe-only and uses completed sentences with regular morphology. The
native-speaker evaluation covers 24 prompts and eight conditions; it supports
the automatic proxy on the tested rubric but not population-scale pragmatic
validity. Grouped CV blocks pair mates, and the extra held-out-role and
pre-marker evaluations improve generalisation evidence without settling it.
Larger human studies, layer-swept intervention, instruction-tuned variants, and
other Indic languages are untested.
